# RL Scaling Laws

Pre-training scaling laws ([[scaling-laws]]) predict loss from parameters, data, and compute. A separate and younger literature asks the analogous question for the RL post-training stage: given a fixed RL compute budget, what asymptotic performance can a recipe reach, and how should that compute be allocated across rollout width, batch size, and optimizer steps? This note covers six resources that answer that question from different angles — two competing functional forms (sigmoidal vs. power-law) for "RL compute → performance," a third paper that turns those forms into prescriptive sampling-allocation rules, NVIDIA's "prolonged training" lineage that established RL scaling empirically before it was named as a law, and a recipe (Polaris) whose released 53K-example dataset became the training set for the largest of these studies. See [[rl-training-systems]] for the rollout/trainer systems these recipes run on, and [[frontier-async-rl]] for the async-execution techniques (CISPO, FP32 LM head) that several of them reuse.

## Two Functional Forms: Sigmoidal vs. Power-Law

The two largest studies in this cluster fit different curves to "performance vs. RL compute" and reach compatible but distinct conclusions.

[The Art of Scaling Reinforcement Learning Compute for LLMs / "ScaleRL" (2510.13786)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.13786) fits a **sigmoidal** curve:

```
R_C − R0 = (A − R0) × 1 / (1 + (C_mid / C)^B)
```

where `A` is the asymptotic performance ceiling, `B` controls compute-efficiency (how fast the curve approaches the asymptote), and `C_mid` is the compute at the inflection point. Meta/UT Austin/UCL/Berkeley/Harvard/Periodic Labs ran over 400,000 GPU-hours to validate this fit, including extrapolating from small runs to an 8B dense model trained for 100,000 GPU-hours (7,400 steps) and a 17B×16 MoE (Llama-4 Scout) trained for 50,000 GPU-hours — in both cases the small-scale fit predicted the large-scale outcome accurately. The central finding is that **most common RL interventions (loss aggregation, normalization, curriculum design, length penalties) change the compute-efficiency term `B`, not the asymptote `A`.** A handful of choices instead shift the ceiling itself — and a recipe that wins at small compute by improving `B` can still lose at scale to a recipe with a higher `A`, an explicit "embracing the Bitter Lesson" framing.

[Scaling Behaviors of LLM Reinforcement Learning Post-Training (2509.25300)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.25300) — not currently in the repo as a PDF; arXiv link only — instead fits a **power law** across the full Qwen2.5 dense family (0.5B to 72B) on mathematical reasoning. Their findings: larger base models are more RL-efficient (more performance gain per unit of RL compute); test loss follows a predictable power law in compute and data; and an efficiency term `k(N)` shows **diminishing — saturating — returns to model scale** as `N` grows, meaning bigger is not unboundedly better for RL efficiency. They also show that in data-constrained regimes, **repeatedly reusing a fixed prompt pool is nearly as good as fresh prompts** — RL performance is governed by optimization steps taken, not by the uniqueness of samples seen, a finding structurally similar to the repeated-data half-life result for pre-training in [[scaling-laws]].

[IsoCompute Playbook: Optimally Scaling Sampling Compute for LLM RL (2603.12151)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12151) — not currently in the repo as a PDF; arXiv link only — explicitly positions itself as the first paper to turn the above two functional forms into **prescriptive allocation rules** rather than just descriptive curves. UC San Diego / MBZUAI-IFM / CMU ran ~120,000 H200-GPU-hours across Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Qwen3-4B-Instruct, and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct using GRPO. They decompose total sampling compute `C = B_p · n · M` into rollouts-per-problem (`n`), problems-per-batch (`B_p`), and optimizer steps (`M`), and find the compute-optimal `n` increases with budget before saturating. Critically, the *mechanism* of improvement is allocation-dependent: easy problems benefit from "solution sharpening" (improving `worst@k`), hard problems benefit from "coverage expansion" (improving `best@k`). At a fixed product `B = B_p · n`, low-compute regimes favor a larger `B_p` (more distinct problems, fewer samples each); high-compute regimes favor a larger `n` (fewer problems, deeper sampling per problem).

## ScaleRL's Recipe and Results

ScaleRL is not just a scaling law — it is also a concrete recipe, assembled by picking the sub-components that move the asymptote `A` upward rather than just the early-compute slope. The components: asynchronous **PipelineRL** execution (see [[rl-training-systems]]) with off-policyness `k=8`, the **CISPO** loss (which beat both DAPO and GSPO on final asymptote, not just early efficiency), **FP32 LM-head logits** (raised `A` from 0.52 to 0.61 — the same fix independently confirmed by MiniMax-M1's training, see [[frontier-async-rl]] and below), prompt-level loss averaging, batch-level advantage normalization, zero-variance filtering, and a "No-Positive-Resampling" curriculum. The recipe was trained on the **Polaris-53K** dataset (a direct reuse of the Polaris recipe's released data — see below) and its final asymptote (`A = 0.61`) surpassed DeepSeek-GRPO, Qwen-DAPO, Magistral, and MiniMax-M1 baselines. ScaleRL also explicitly benchmarks itself against NVIDIA's ProRL, training roughly 3.5× longer than ProRL's published run.

## The ProRL Lineage: Prolonged Training as an Empirical Precursor

NVIDIA's "ProRL" line of work established the practical version of an RL scaling law — "training longer keeps helping, if you can keep it stable" — before either of the two functional-form papers above formalized it.

[Scaling Up RL: Unlocking Diverse Reasoning in LLMs via Prolonged Training / "ProRL" (2507.12507)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.12507) — not currently in the repo as a PDF; arXiv link only — is the V1 paper. NVIDIA trained DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B on 4 nodes × 8 H100-80GB (~16,000 GPU-hours) using `verl`, combining GRPO with DAPO-style enhancements (decoupled clip, `ε_low=0.2` / `ε_high=0.4`, dynamic sampling), a small KL penalty (`β=0.0001`), and — the key enabler of "prolonged" training — **periodic hard resets of the reference policy** whenever KL spiked or validation plateaued. The run was structured as 8 sequential "Runs," each fixing a specific stability failure mode encountered in the previous one. Results against the un-RL'd baseline: +14.7% math, +13.9% coding, +54.8% logic puzzles, +25.1% STEM, +18.1% instruction-following. A secondary finding worth keeping: a high rollout temperature (1.2 vs. the more common 0.6) kept helping even late into training, where lower-temperature runs had already plateaued.

*ProRL V2 — Prolonged Training Validates RL Scaling Laws* (NVIDIA, [Notion post](https://hijkzzz.notion.site/prorl-v2)) is the named V2 sequel and is the resource that gives this cluster its explicit "RL scaling laws" framing. **The Notion page itself could not be retrieved** — it serves only a client-rendered JavaScript shell with no extractable text, and this held across multiple URL variants. The only available descriptions of V2's content are secondhand (a GitHub paper-notes tracker and a Medium summary), and they are **not independently verified here**. What can be said with confidence: it is NVIDIA's sequel to ProRL V1, extending prolonged training further and tying the results explicitly to RL scaling-law claims. Specific numbers or techniques attributed to it beyond that should be treated as unconfirmed until the primary source is reachable — do not cite secondhand specifics as fact.

## Polaris: Calibrated Difficulty, Diversity Sampling, and the ScaleRL Dataset Link

[POLARIS: A POst-training recipe for scaling reinforcement Learning on Advanced ReasonIng modelS](https://hkunlp.github.io/blog/2025/Polaris/) (HKU NLP Group, ByteDance Seed, Fudan) is a recipe-and-dataset release rather than an arXiv scaling-law paper, but it belongs in this cluster for two reasons: it makes its own scaling claims about small reasoning models, and **its released 53K-example dataset is the training set ScaleRL uses** — a direct, concrete link between the two largest studies here.

Polaris trains Polaris-4B-Preview and Polaris-7B-Preview, with the 4B model reaching 81.2% AIME24 / 79.4% AIME25 — beating Claude-4-Opus and Grok-3-Beta, and matching Qwen3-235B-A22B on AIME25 at under 2% of the parameter count. Four pillars drive this: (1) **calibrated data difficulty** — a "mirror effect" finding that small models need a difficulty distribution mirrored relative to what works for large models, built by merging DeepScaleR (40K→26K) with AReaL-boba into the 53K/30K training sets that ScaleRL later reuses; (2) **diversity-based rollout sampling** across three temperature zones (initializing at the edge of the "Coverage-Exploration Zone" and increasing temperature across stages, e.g., 1.4→1.45→1.5); (3) **inference-time length extrapolation via YaRN**, which boosts accuracy on >32K-token generations from ~26% to over 50%, continuing to improve out to 96K tokens; and (4) **exploration efficiency** mechanisms (a "Rollout Rescue Mechanism" and "Intra-Batch Informative Substitution"). The recipe builds on DAPO's Clip-High mechanism and removes the entropy and KL loss terms entirely.

## Cross-Links Across This Cluster

Three concrete links are worth tracking explicitly when navigating between these resources: Polaris's 53K dataset is reused verbatim as ScaleRL's training data; ScaleRL benchmarks directly against ProRL V1 (training ~3.5× longer); and ScaleRL's FP32-LM-head finding (`A`: 0.52→0.61) is independently corroborated by MiniMax-M1's training run, documented in [[frontier-async-rl]]. The IsoCompute Playbook explicitly cites both ScaleRL and Scaling Behaviors as its only two functional-form predecessors, and frames its own contribution as turning their descriptive curves into a prescriptive sampling-allocation policy.

## Ring-Zero: What Changes at One Trillion Parameters

**Ring-Zero** scales zero-shot reinforcement learning from a 104B-parameter comparison model to a **one-trillion-parameter MoE**. Its contribution is as much systems engineering as algorithm design: a pipeline-aware clipped-ratio objective prevents stale rollout policies from producing destructive updates, a training–inference correction narrows numerical drift between the two execution stacks, and a staged curriculum expands context from 4K to 64K tokens before self-distillation and sample-level filtering sharpen the final policy. The first training stage reaches **84.2 on AIME 2026**, versus **78.0** for the 104B model; the second stage raises the trillion-parameter model to **93.2**.

The scale exposes a useful failure mode: reasoning length has **inertia**. Average outputs grow from 2,353 tokens on low-difficulty problems to 8,085 on medium and 20,817 on high difficulty, but once the policy learns long traces it does not automatically become concise again. The authors therefore separate a **discovery phase**, where long rollouts find new solution paths, from a **sharpening phase**, where self-distillation and filtered samples consolidate them. This is an important correction to a simple “more RL compute is always better” story: rollout length, problem difficulty, numerical consistency, and the policy-update pipeline must be co-designed at trillion-parameter scale.
