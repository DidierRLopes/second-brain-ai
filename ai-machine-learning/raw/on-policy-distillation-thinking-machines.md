# On-Policy Distillation

**Source:** Thinking Machines blog (https://thinkingmachines.ai/)
**Author:** Kevin Lu, in collaboration with others at Thinking Machines
**Published:** October 27, 2025
**Implementation:** Tinker cookbook (https://thinkingmachines.ai/tinker/)

## Key Concepts

LLMs reach expert performance via a stack of training stages: pre-training (general capacities), mid-training (domain knowledge), post-training (targeted behavior). Smaller, well-trained models often outperform larger generalist models in their trained domains, but unlocking that requires choosing the right post-training method.

Post-training methods divide along two axes:
- **Sampling distribution:** on-policy (student samples its own rollouts) vs. off-policy (student learns to imitate target outputs from an external source).
- **Reward signal:** sparse (one bit per episode) vs. dense (per-token signal).

| Method | Sampling | Reward signal |
|---|---|---|
| Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) | off-policy | dense |
| Reinforcement learning (RL) | on-policy | sparse |
| **On-policy distillation (OPD)** | **on-policy** | **dense** |

OPD combines the on-policy relevance of RL with the dense reward signal of distillation: sample trajectories from the student, then use a high-performing teacher to grade each token via per-token reverse KL.

## The Sport Analogy

- **On-policy RL** is like playing chess games with no coaching — feedback is tied to your own play, but it's received only at the end of a match and doesn't tell you which moves mattered.
- **Off-policy distillation** is like watching a grandmaster play — extremely strong moves, but in board states a novice will rarely find themselves in.
- **On-policy distillation** is like a coach who labels each of *your own* moves on a "blunder → brilliant" scale (cf. chess.com analysis). On-policy, dense, and the labels are tied to states you actually visit.

## Implementation

The post implements OPD on top of the Tinker RL API. Key choices:

**Loss: per-token reverse KL.**

```
KL(π_θ || π_teacher) = E_{x ∼ π_θ} [ log π_θ(x_{t+1} | x_{1..t}) − log π_teacher(x_{t+1} | x_{1..t}) ]
```

The student is pushed to approximate the teacher's behavior in every state the student finds itself in. Discount factor = 0 (only optimize the immediate next token; >0 gave no practical improvement).

**Why reverse KL.**
- *Mode-seeking.* Learns one specific behavior (the teacher's) rather than spreading across suboptimal options.
- *"Unhackable."* Low KL always corresponds to high probability of desirable behavior from the teacher's POV.
- *Reduces exposure bias.* Unlike off-policy distillation.

**Compute savings.**
- No rollout needs to finish before computing reward → can use shorter/partial rollouts.
- Querying teacher logprobs is a single forward pass on the large model — *prefill*, much cheaper than generation.
- Trajectories are generated by the smaller, cheaper student.
- No separate reward or labeling model required.

**Pseudocode (the change vs. an RL implementation with KL regularization is essentially one line — swap the regularizer model for the teacher).**

```python
# 1. Initialize teacher client
teacher_client = service_client.create_sampling_client(base_model=teacher_config.base_model)

# 2. Sample trajectories from student
trajectories = do_group_rollout(student_client, env_group_builder)
sampled_logprobs = trajectories.loss_fn_inputs["logprobs"]

# 3. Compute reward (reverse KL)
teacher_logprobs = teacher_client.compute_logprobs(trajectories)
reverse_kl = sampled_logprobs - teacher_logprobs
trajectories["advantages"] = -reverse_kl

# 4. Train with RL importance-sampling loss
training_client.forward_backward(trajectories, loss_fn="importance_sampling")
```

**Illustrative example.** A failing student trajectory on a SimpleBench question (ice cubes in a frying pan — answer is 0). The teacher (Qwen3-235B-A22B) penalizes tokens that *start phrases which lead the student astray* — the "forking tokens" that guide reasoning. The final wrong answer isn't heavily penalized; it's predictable given the preceding sequence.

## Distillation for Reasoning (Qwen3-8B-Base, math)

Teacher: Qwen3-32B. Student: Qwen3-8B-Base. Both supported on Tinker.

**Off-policy distillation (SFT on OpenThoughts-3) as baseline.** Full fine-tuning on 400k prompts reaches 60% AIME'24. Performance scales log-linearly with prompts. Extrapolating: ~70% at ~2M prompts (consistent with OpenThoughts-3 and DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B published results).

**RL baseline (from Qwen3 technical report).** 67.6% on AIME'24 using 17,920 GPU hours on top of similar SFT init.

**On-policy distillation.** Starting from the 400k SFT checkpoint, OPD reaches 70% AIME'24 in ~150 steps (~77k prompts × 4 samples).

| Method | AIME'24 | GPQA-Diamond | GPU Hours |
|---|---|---|---|
| Off-policy distillation | 55.0% | 55.6% | unreported |
| + Reinforcement learning | 67.6% | 61.3% | 17,920 |
| + On-policy distillation | 74.4% | 63.3% | 1,800 |

From Qwen3 Technical Report, Table 21.

**Compute efficiency.**

| Method | AIME'24 | Teacher FLOPs | Student FLOPs | CE vs SFT-2M |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Init: SFT-400K | 60% | 8.5×10²⁰ | 3.8×10²⁰ | — |
| SFT-2M (extrapolated) | ~70% | 3.4×10²¹ | 1.5×10²¹ | 1× |

- **9× savings** if the SFT dataset is "given" (amortized across runs).
- **~30× savings** if you include the full cost of sampling from the teacher (i.e., comparing apples to apples on a new task with no pre-existing SFT dataset).
- LoRA performance gap to full FT shrinks under OPD: 13% gap after SFT vs. 6% gap after OPD.

## Distillation for Personalization (Internal Assistant)

A common production goal: a model that combines new domain knowledge (e.g., internal company documents) with strong post-training behavior (instruction following, chat). Two evals:
1. **Internal QA eval** — does the model know domain facts?
2. **IF-eval** — does it follow instructions?

**The problem: midtraining degrades post-trained behavior.** Starting from Qwen3-8B (already strongly post-trained), fine-tuning on internal docs causes catastrophic forgetting of instruction following. Mixing in "background data" from the original pretraining distribution is a classic fix — but Qwen3's pretraining data isn't available.

**Stronger baseline: chat-data background.** Take Tulu3 prompts and re-sample them with Qwen3-8B itself (acts as a forward-KL regularizer toward the model's own distribution). Sampling from Qwen3-8B preserves chat capabilities better than sampling from Qwen3-32B (highlighting the data-source sensitivity; cf. Chen et al. 2025 on retaining-by-doing).

**Sweeping document : chat ratio.** Increasing document-data share improves knowledge straightforwardly. At least 30% chat helps preserve most instruction-following ability. **But no weighting fully recovers the original IF-eval.** This is true even for 100% chat data.

**LoRA.** Constrains parameter updates but is *also* insufficient — LoRA learns less (knowledge) and still forgets post-training behavior.

**On-policy distillation as the recovery step.** After fine-tuning on a 70/30 doc/chat mix, run OPD with Qwen3-8B itself as teacher on Tulu3 prompts (no document data in this phase). The earlier model is the teacher to "re-invoke" lost capabilities.

| Model | Internal QA | IF-eval |
|---|---|---|
| Qwen3-8B | 18% | 85% |
| + midtrain (100%) | 43% | 45% |
| + midtrain (70%) | 36% | 79% |
| **+ midtrain (70%) + distill** | **41%** | **83%** |

OPD recovers nearly full IF-eval performance *without losing knowledge*. There's even some positive transfer (knowledge eval goes up after distillation).

The framing: any instruction-tuned open-weight model can be used as a "reward model" via `compute_logprobs`. High-probability behaviors under that teacher are implicitly the rewards. Connects to DPO ("your language model is secretly a reward model") and inverse RL.

## Discussion: Why OPD Is So Efficient

**Density of reward.** From the *LoRA Without Regret* perspective: RL teaches O(1) bits per episode, distillation teaches O(N) bits per episode where N is sequence length. The post quantifies this with a controlled experiment:

1. Start with Qwen3-8B-Base.
2. Run RL on DeepMath (LoRA rank 128).
3. On-policy distill from the RL-trained checkpoint back to the base.

OPD learns the RL-trained policy in **7–10× fewer gradient steps**, which corresponds to **50–100× lower compute** (since OPD also enables shorter contexts and smaller batches). Reverse KL → near-zero and AIME score recovered in <10 gradient steps, vs. 70 for RL.

**Data efficiency.** With RL, multi-epoch training on the same prompt often leads to memorizing the final answer. OPD learns to approximate the teacher's *complete distribution* via reverse KL — many samples per prompt remain useful. Demonstrated: training Qwen3-8B-Base on a *single math prompt* for 20 consecutive steps × 256 rollouts approximately matches teacher AIME'24 performance.

## Interpretation: What RL Actually Does

> RL doesn't spend most of its compute on gradient steps. It spends most of its compute on *search* — rolling out a policy and assigning credit.

- **Pre-training** is exploration in high-dimensional parameter space. Cost is in the gradient steps themselves.
- **RL** is exploration in the space of *semantic strategies*. At every step it tries a small modification of some strategy it has found. It "stumbles" onto new strategies by luck — sampling from the set of weights it already has. Once a strategy is found, **distillation is a shortcut for transferring it**. OPD doesn't need to model the intermediate strategies during the RL curriculum — just the final strategy.

Analogy: in science, finding a result takes years; teaching it in natural language takes minutes. Intuitive physical skills (sports) are the opposite — they live in muscle memory, hard to transfer except by repeated practice. RL → strategies (transferable). Pre-training → parameters (not).

## On-Policy Learning as a Tool for Continual Learning

Prior work (RL's Razor; Shenfeld et al. 2025): on-policy learning forgets less than off-policy. But RL can't *teach new knowledge* well, so it's not sufficient for continual learning. SFT (including off-policy distillation) is the natural complement but degrades behavior.

**A surprising failure mode of SFT, even with KL=0 data.** Take Tulu3 prompts, sample from Qwen3-32B at T=1.0 (no modification). The dataset has KL=0 against Qwen3-32B in expectation. *But running SFT on this dataset still degrades IF-eval performance for any non-zero learning rate.*

Mechanism: while KL is zero in expectation, every finite batch has a slightly different distribution. Training on these finite batches causes non-zero gradient updates that diverge the updated model from its original state. Training on one's own samples becomes off-policy over time → exposure bias → error accumulation on long sequences.

**OPD's continual-learning property.** OPD *always stays on-policy*. Since the teacher is fixed, the student converges on the teacher's desirable behavior without the SFT-style regression — even in the self-distillation setting. This makes it a strong primitive for alternating phases of "learn new knowledge" + "recover behavior."

## Conclusion

OPD is a cheap, powerful post-training primitive. It combines on-policy state coverage with dense, per-token supervision. Reported gains over RL: ~50–100× compute efficiency in matched-teacher setups, ~9–30× vs. extrapolated off-policy distillation. The continual-learning property — staying on-policy keeps the student from drifting — is particularly valuable for updating deployed models with new knowledge without regressing trained behavior.
