# RL Systems Mind the Gap: Matching Trainer and Generator Throughput (SemiAnalysis)

**Source:** https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/rl-systems-mind-the-gap-matching
**Authors:** Kimbo Chen, Cheang Kang Wen, Dylan Patel
**Published:** June 16, 2026
**Filed under:** RL training infrastructure

## Premise

RL post-training is what elicits agentic-coding capability from pretrained models, and its cost makes RL system efficiency a first-order constraint on how far capability can go. The article frames RL training-system efficiency as a **throughput-matching problem** between two actors — the generator (inference) and the trainer (backprop) — grounded in real experiments on open frameworks (Prime RL, slime, verl) and open models (Qwen3-235B variants, GLM-5).

## The Three Actors

- **Generator** — runs inference on dataset prompts, producing rollouts (prompt + response).
- **RL environment / sandbox** — scores rollouts (e.g. executes code, runs tests) and returns rewards. Implemented as Firecracker micro-VMs up to full QEMU VMs; sandbox-as-a-service vendors (Modal) optimize startup latency via content-addressed caching.
- **Trainer** — ingests rollouts + rewards, computes gradients, pushes new weights back to the generator.

## GRPO, Just Enough

GRPO samples a group of rollouts per prompt, computes each rollout's advantage relative to the group average, and reinforces above-average / suppresses below-average rollouts. If every rollout in a group gets the same reward (task too easy or too hard — solve rate near 100% or 0%), every advantage is zero and the group contributes no training signal. This **reward-distribution** constraint drives group-size and curriculum decisions.

## Synchronous → Asynchronous RL

Classic policy-gradient algorithms assume all rollouts in a group share one policy version, forcing the generator to finish a batch before the trainer can update weights — synchronous execution wastes compute. **PipelineRL** (arXiv 2509.19128) introduces in-flight weight updates: the trainer pushes new weights while rollouts are still being generated, overlapping trainer and generator execution. The cost is **policy staleness** — each sample is generated by a mixture of old and new policy weights.

- Staleness occurs at three granularities: **trajectory-level** (rollout started under policy t, consumed at t+k), **token-level** (in-flight updates change which policy generates which part of a single rollout), and **environment state-level** (see Partial Rollout below).
- A **policy-staleness budget** bounds how far the generator can run ahead of the trainer before samples are rejected — this is what lets generator and trainer run at different speeds. PipelineRL is described as "a throughput-matching scheme with bounded policy staleness," now the de facto open-source implementation.

## The Throughput-Matching Framework

Models the system as a producer/consumer queue: generator produces rollouts into a queue, trainer consumes from it.

- **Generation-bound**: generator slower than trainer → queue empties → trainer idles between steps.
- **Trainer-bound** (less common in their experiments): generator faster → queue grows → samples age → staleness issues.

**Trainer consumption rate** = samples per step / training step time. Step count depends on group size, reward-distribution/advantage-filtering (dropping zero-advantage samples), and minimum effective batch size; step time depends on model size/precision and parallelism/memory configuration (TP/PP/DP/EP, FSDP, offloading, activation checkpointing).

**Generator production rate** = concurrent rollouts / end-to-end latency, further reduced by an **acceptance rate** (not all generated samples are consumed) via:
- **Early pruning** (arXiv 2603.24840v1) — abandon rollouts early based on length caps, value functions, or intermediate verifier checks.
- **Adaptive sampling** — e.g. online advantage filtering (Prime Intellect's Intellect-2 blog).

End-to-end latency is driven by inference throughput (parallelism, KV-cache quantization, PD disaggregation, speculative decoding), sandbox latency (startup + execution), reward-modeling type (cheap verifiers vs. slow LLM judges), and reward shape (per-turn PRM vs. full-rollout reward).

Concurrency is capped by aggregate KV-cache memory ÷ average sequence length. **Straggler** rollouts (the longest in a group) set the group's completion time, motivating oversampling and partial rollout (below).

## Model Capability and Behavior as System Variables

**Solve rate** (% of a group's rollouts that pass verification) near 0% or 100% collapses the reward distribution to uniform → zero advantage → no signal. This shapes group size, advantage-filtering strategy, and **curriculum** (task ordering, tuned to keep solve rate in a productive middle band). Longer CoT outputs increase KV-cache use and reduce max concurrency; more tool calls increase sandbox time and strain sandbox infra. Both capability and behavior drift over the course of training, so the system has to keep adapting.

## Case Studies (all agentic coding, MoE models, ≥10 nodes)

1. **Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking-2507, Prime RL + Mini SWE Agent Plus.** Long-response variance drives severe tail latency, forcing 60% oversampling-discard. Curriculum slightly too hard initially — zero-solution rollout groups spike after step 1 before recovering. Net: generation-bound; trainer at 2.75 samples/s, 30% idle, 10.5% MFU vs. generator's 1.95 samples/s using 3× trainer compute.
2. **GLM-5, Prime RL, PD-disaggregated generator.** Response length and tool calls (20→51) triple over the run, shifting workload prefill-heavy (justifying PD disaggregation). Curriculum too easy — 55% of problems hit 100% solve rate → flat average reward. Trainer idles 74% of wall-clock; its consumption rate is 5× the generator's delivered production rate.
3. **Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct, verl + uni-agent, Modal sandboxes, GRPO.** Generation-bound (30–60% trainer idle); tail latency up to 7500s at 960 concurrent rollouts due to sandbox initialization-dead errors and 1-hour spin-up stragglers — scaled back to 96 concurrent rollouts to mitigate. Direct empirical demonstration that sandbox-infra reliability, not just algorithmic design, gates achievable concurrency.
4. **Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking-2507-FP8, slime + GSPO, SWE-Bench Verified/Lite, Modal, partial rollout.** 60% trainer wait, 12K-token responses, 12 tool calls/sequence. Highlights **partial rollout**: straggler rollouts are aborted, saved to a replay buffer, and resumed in a later batch instead of being discarded outright, trading stateful-sandbox complexity for reduced tail-latency waste.

## Partial Rollout and Stateful Sandbox Design

Partial rollout requires the sandbox to **persist across batches** (e.g. half-applied file edits in a SWE-Bench repo), be **lazily created** (avoid wasting a sandbox on a sample that never emits a tool call), and be **robust to failure** during the abort→resume window. slime triggers abortion when a target rollout batch fills or when the trainer pushes a weight update; aborted rollouts evict KV cache (unlike PipelineRL), so resumption becomes a large prefill request — exactly the workload PD disaggregation helps with.

This surfaces **environment state-level staleness**: when a resumed rollout's sandbox holds edits/files from an *older* policy, the *current* policy must continue from a state it didn't create, and the resulting advantage gets attributed to a trajectory the current policy only partially owns — a training-signal corruption distinct from token- or trajectory-level staleness.

## Software Notes (qualitative)

- **Prime RL**: good ergonomics (uv, .toml configs, agent skill files), orchestrator actor abstraction, Environments Hub, PD disaggregation support. Rough edges: heavy uv dependence, SLURM/bare-metal assumption causing CUDA/DeepGEMM version mismatches, opaque Prime Sandbox failures (still beta).
- **slime**: clean minimal hook abstractions, responsive maintainers; documentation gap specifically around async/fully-async modes and partial rollout mechanics (learned mostly via trial and error).
- **Modal**: strong API docs, reliable at small scale; concurrent-sandbox dead-init errors and long-tail startup traced to an account resource limit, resolved after Modal raised it.

## History of Open-Source RL Frameworks

DeepSeek R1's release triggered the open-source RL-infra wave. **OpenRLHF** (PPO, REINFORCE++, then GRPO) was an early influential framework; many of its maintainers went on to build **slime** and **verl**, seeding vibrant RL-training communities (credited with contributing to Chinese open-model progress) and giving academic researchers practical access to RL-systems research.

## Conclusion

RL training-system efficiency reduces to **queue health**: oversampling, early pruning, and partial rollout control what *enters* the queue; adaptive sampling and the policy-staleness budget control what *leaves* it; in-flight weight updates let trainer and generator run at different speeds. The piece frames this as much an infrastructure problem as an algorithmic one. (A paid section compares TCO against Thinking Machines' Tinker platform — not accessible without subscription.)
